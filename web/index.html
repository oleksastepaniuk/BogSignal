The landscape

Bogs are among the most valuable ecosystems we have, and among the most overlooked. They take thousands of years to form from just three ingredients: peat, water and plants.

A bog's superpower is holding water. Sphagnum moss soaks it up and keeps it from evaporating, so a bog holds more water than a lake of the same size. That makes bogs quiet workhorses:

  • Flood control. They absorb rain and meltwater and release it slowly into rivers and lakes.
  • Clean water. Peat is a natural filter. Pollutants settle in it as water seeps through.
  • Carbon storage. Peat stores carbon built up over millennia. Drain a bog and that carbon escapes as greenhouse gases.

Polissia, Europe's Amazon

Most of Ukraine's bogs lie in Polissia, mainly in the Rivne, Volyn and Chernihiv regions, and some are 5,000 to 12,000 years old. Polissia is Europe's largest intact floodplain region. It covers 186,000 km² across Ukraine, Belarus, Poland and Russia, and it is so flat that its altitude never varies by more than 150 metres. The 750 km-long Prypiat, one of Europe's most pristine rivers, winds through it towards the Dnipro. Along the way its meanders and oxbows form a maze of floodplains, peatlands, forests, bogs, marshes and lakes.

It is full of life. More than 60% of the world's aquatic warblers, Europe's rarest migratory songbird, depend on Polissia, and every spring millions of migrating birds stop on its floodplains to rest and refuel. European bison, brown bears, grey wolves and lynx live in its forests. About two thirds of the world's waterwheel plants, a rare carnivorous water plant, grow in the exclusion zone around Chornobyl. In Ukraine alone, 19,692 km² of Polissia is protected as Emerald Network sites, Europe's network of areas of special conservation interest.

Bringing the bogs back

Over the last 100 years, Ukraine has drained more than 1 million hectares of bogs, and the draining goes on. A drained bog dries out and its peat catches fire easily. Peat fires can smoulder for years, and drainage also brings dust storms and floods on nearby land. From above, a drained bog can pass for forest or farmland, and some living wetlands are hidden under trees.

Now some of that damage is being undone. The Rivne Nature Reserve has begun restoring the natural water regime of two of its bog massifs, Somyne and Syra Pohonia, which together cover about 20,000 hectares. For decades, drainage canals carried water away from these bogs. Groundwater fell, the vegetation changed and the risk of peat fires grew. The aim now is to keep the water in. It is Ukraine's first comprehensive bog restoration on this scale, supported by the Frankfurt Zoological Society through the Wild Polissia programme. The main works are due to finish by the end of November 2026.

To check whether it is working, the reserve has installed 17 automatic sensors that track groundwater levels and set up 30 study plots to monitor vegetation and water chemistry. The first changes may show within a few seasons, but full recovery of the bogs will take much longer.

Our case-study sites

We study three sites in western Ukrainian Polissia: the two bogs being restored in the Rivne Nature Reserve, and Cheremske in Volyn region.

Somyne

Rivne Nature Reserve, Rivne region

A bog massif where restoration is well under way. Seven earth dams now hold back water in the drainage canals, and work on the culverts is wrapping up. Next, three wooden sluice gates will go into hard-to-reach parts of the reserve so the water level in the canals can be controlled.

Syra Pohonia

Rivne Nature Reserve, Rivne region

A bog massif with open transition mires. Work starts once the main drainage canal is handed over to the reserve, which the State Property Fund of Ukraine has already approved. The plan is two culverts, ten earth dams and twelve wooden sluice gates.

Cheremske

Cheremske Nature Reserve, Volyn region

Our third site lies in Volyn region, one of the parts of Polissia that holds most of Ukraine's bogs. It is not part of the Rivne restoration project.
