Europe’s Amazon

Across a roughly 186,000 km² region, Polissia is a mosaic of rivers, floodplains, peatlands and forests — one of Europe’s great wetland landscapes. These habitats store carbon, regulate water and provide vital refuge for wildlife and migratory birds.

Why bogs matter

Bogs are among the most valuable ecosystems we have, and among the most overlooked. They take thousands of years to form from just three ingredients: peat, water and plants.

A bog's superpower is holding water. Sphagnum moss soaks it up and keeps it from evaporating, so a bog holds more water than a lake of the same size. That makes bogs quiet workhorses:

  • Flood control. They absorb rain and meltwater and release it slowly into rivers and lakes.
  • Clean water. Peat is a natural filter. Pollutants settle in it as water seeps through.
  • Carbon storage. Peat stores carbon built up over millennia. Drain a bog and that carbon escapes as greenhouse gases.

Where Ukraine’s bogs are

Most of Ukraine's bogs lie in Polissia, mainly in the Rivne, Volyn and Chernihiv regions, and some are 5,000 to 12,000 years old. Polissia is Europe's largest intact floodplain region. It stretches across Ukraine, Belarus, Poland and Russia, and it is so flat that its altitude never varies by more than 150 metres. The 750 km-long Prypiat, one of Europe's most pristine rivers, winds through it towards the Dnipro. Along the way its meanders and oxbows form a maze of floodplains, peatlands, forests, bogs, marshes and lakes.

It is full of life. More than 60% of the world's aquatic warblers, Europe's rarest migratory songbird, depend on Polissia, and every spring millions of migrating birds stop on its floodplains to rest and refuel. European bison, brown bears, grey wolves and lynx live in its forests. About two thirds of the world's waterwheel plants, a rare carnivorous water plant, grow in the exclusion zone around Chornobyl. In Ukraine alone, 19,692 km² of Polissia is protected as Emerald Network sites, Europe's network of areas of special conservation interest.

Bringing the bogs back

Decades of drainage, agricultural expansion and infrastructure development have put Polissia's wetlands under pressure. Over the last 100 years, Ukraine has drained more than 1 million hectares of bogs, and the draining goes on. A drained bog dries out and its peat catches fire easily. Peat fires can smoulder for years, and drainage also brings dust storms and floods on nearby land. From above, a drained bog can pass for forest or farmland, and some living wetlands are hidden under trees.

But efforts to reverse this damage are underway. Conservation initiatives such as Wild Polissia are working to bring water back to drained wetlands, aiming to restore up to 20,000 hectares in Ukraine alone.

What inspired us

In the Rivne Nature Reserve, work has begun to restore the natural water regime of two bog massifs, Somyne and Syra Pohonia. For decades, drainage canals carried water away from these bogs. Groundwater fell, the vegetation changed and the risk of peat fires grew. The aim now is to keep the water in. It is Ukraine's first comprehensive bog restoration on this scale, supported by the Frankfurt Zoological Society through the Wild Polissia programme, and the main works are due to finish by the end of November 2026.

To check whether it is working, the reserve has installed 17 automatic sensors that track groundwater levels and set up 30 study plots to monitor vegetation and water chemistry. The first changes may show within a few seasons, but full recovery of the bogs will take much longer.

Sensors and study plots show in detail what is happening at two bogs. But Polissia covers roughly 186,000 km², and before more bogs can be brought back, someone has to find them: which ones are drained, which still hold water and which are hidden under trees. No team can survey all of that on foot, but satellites pass over the whole region again and again. BogSignal asks whether satellite data, and the new NISAR radar in particular, can help conservation programmes, reserve staff and researchers decide where to look first.

We study three sites in western Ukrainian Polissia: Somyne and Syra Pohonia, where restoration is under way, and Cheremske, a protected reserve in Volyn region outside the restoration project.

Seeing beyond the surface

To protect wetlands, we first need to understand where they are — and be able to follow how they change. Satellites can cover enormous areas repeatedly, but clouds and vegetation can conceal important clues.

We tested whether NISAR could add useful information to existing satellite observations, as a first step toward a stronger baseline for future monitoring.

Sentinel-2

What the landscape looks like

Reflected sunlight reveals vegetation, open water and land cover. Clouds block the view, and a canopy can hide water below.

Sentinel-1 · C-band

A different perspective

Radar works day and night through clouds. Backscatter responds to surface roughness, moisture and vegetation structure.

NISAR · L-band

Looking deeper into vegetation

NISAR’s longer-wavelength radar can penetrate further into some vegetation canopies, providing additional clues about woody structure and water beneath vegetation. These signals alone do not confirm a bog.

02 — Knowing where to look first

The highest-scoring 5% of our validation area contained 73.4% of the swamp and marsh pixels in our reference maps. This suggests BogSignal could help researchers narrow their search, prioritizing promising areas for closer investigation.

A first step toward better-informed conservation

BogSignal can help focus wetland research on places worth a closer look. The next steps are independent checks of promising locations and repeated observations to investigate change. Identifying wetland signals does not establish restoration suitability.

Full results & methods

We used U-Net with a ResNet-34 encoder initialized from ImageNet weights. The encoder extracts image features, while the decoder combines them with finer spatial detail to predict a class for each pixel. The same architecture and training recipe were used for each sensor combination, with input channels adapted to the selected sensors and their validity masks. The model predicts six classes; for the displayed wetland results, Swamp and Marsh probabilities are added before choosing among five combined classes.

Detailed experiment results

A-D share 585 validation patches and 37,411,425 scored pixels. Wetland means Swamp + Marsh; water and peatland classes remain separate.

IoU measures overlap. Recall measures reference wetlands recovered; reference precision measures agreement among predicted wetlands. The temporal follow-up has a smaller matched footprint: compare TD only with the matched D values in its row, not directly with A-D above. TD averages NISAR linear power over 3-5 observations before dB conversion; processing, dates and training populations also differ. It improved reference precision but reduced recall, so D remains highlighted.

Comparison
The sensor comparison asks whether adding NISAR improves agreement with weak reference labels.

Limitations

  • Single seed (17), validation against historical/inferred weak references. Labels can be incomplete, wrong or temporally mismatched; Other is not verified dry land.
  • Validation informed checkpoint selection. No test-set results or independent field validation are available.
  • Review budget means scored area, not a count of sites. Scores are uncalibrated and do not confirm wetlands.
  • Examples are deliberately selected by performance, not a representative sample. Little effect means similar wetland IoU; maps can still differ.
  • These experiments do not establish restoration suitability or verified discovery of new wetland sites.
